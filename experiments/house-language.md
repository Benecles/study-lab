# Study Lab — emerging house language

Status: working hypotheses, not a final design system. Updated 2026-09-07 by Codex.

## What the owner has actually endorsed

The folio's paper identity, subject-specific illustrations, subtle subject colors, smooth motion and self-drawing plant received positive feedback. The blueprint subject was praised as a surprising but coherent interpretation of paper. The folio itself is not a settled homepage format. The gray surround and redundant explanatory labels were questioned, not ordered removed.

Earlier Claude experiments established useful negative evidence: paper collage can become a campus bulletin board despite good individual details; age spots can read as a dirty screen; uniform dots over everything can destroy paper identity. Ranking an unsuccessful option above another is not endorsement of its direction.

## Vocabulary — ingredients are not recipes

- Substrates: warm opaque stock, tracing paper, colored print stock, technical drawing paper.
- Marks: pencil contour, typed caption, ink fill, selective halftone, registration mark, handwritten annotation.
- Construction: cut edge, displaced sheet, hinge/tape, fixed disc over rotating disc.
- Motion: drawing a mark, lifting a layer, aligning a subject with a reading position.
- Typography: Fraunces for display, Crimson Pro for reading, Courier Prime for instrument labels. Reuse supports family resemblance but is not sufficient to establish it.

## Syntax — the relationships under test

| Relationship | Current evidence | Boundary / failure |
|---|---|---|
| Layer = a distinct reading of shared geometry | Tracing experiment keeps terrain, occupation and memory separately inspectable | Stacked translucency reduces contrast; keep long prose outside the stack |
| Ink = large graphic form | Blue/coral overprint produces a third region, while body text remains clean | Dark overlap can become muddy; do not texture all text |
| Mechanism = navigation | Volvelle aligns an item to a fixed reading position | Center disc initially hid labels; moving parts must not obscure the index |
| Material = local to its object | Texture follows its sheet; removed sheets change visible composite | Screen-fixed stains/texture can look like monitor defects |
| Movement = a change the reader can explain | Lifting removes a layer; rotation selects another subject | Motion should not be the only way to access content |
| Variation = composition as well as palette | These three candidates differ in organizing behavior and graphic weight | A recolored card grid does not establish a different world |

## What belongs, provisionally

A new component should contribute to a coherent material/structural idea, fit the surrounding scale and contrast, remain readable at actual viewing size, and survive a phone-width composition. Physical realism is optional; physical intelligibility is useful. A paper object can be stylized, exaggerated, or animated beyond literal paper behavior.

These are evaluation questions, not admission rules. A surprising experiment may earn a place by contradicting them convincingly.

## How to continue with another designer or AI

1. Read the owner's feedback in the main notebook before copying a visual recipe.
2. Pick a question, not only a legal subject. Legal content can be placeholder material for a visual test; do not invent doctrine and present it as instruction.
3. Find a concrete source and distinguish what it shows from your adaptation.
4. Record the first hypothesis before rendering.
5. Inspect an actual wide and narrow rendering, exercise the interaction, and record discrepancies between intention and result.
6. Change the relevant variable; note the before/after evidence. Do not fabricate a tidy iteration story.
7. Ask the owner a few specific experiential questions. Record their words separately from your interpretation.
8. Preserve useful techniques from rejected compositions, and keep uncertainty visible.
